CodenameHAZEN

The 5 skills that get a designer the image they actually wanted.

Most of us describe a picture and hope. Hazen makes the design decisions first, so the image model makes what you pictured, not just something close.

  1. Think, quick hazen-brainstorm-light A quick, clear answer to a small question.
  2. Think, deep hazen-brainstorming Works through the big questions with you.
  3. Direct hazen-prompt-optimizer Turns your decision into clear instructions.
  4. Make, images hazen-generate-img Picks the right model and makes the image.
  5. Make, video hazen-generate-video Does the same for video.

The hard part now is saying what you want.

Image models can make almost anything. The trouble is the gap between what you mean and what you actually type.

What you picture

  • a restaurant room, from inside
  • expensive, but not cold
  • warm, and not in the lighting sense
  • one long table, mid-meal
  • light pooled on the wood
  • people have just left the frame

The dashed parts never make it into words.

What you type

luxury family restaurant interior, warm and premium, high end but family friendly, 8k, ultra detailed, hyperrealistic, masterpiece, award winning, trending on artstation

3 decided9 left to the model

What you get

So you re-roll.

The first image is close, but not right. So you add a word, then five more, and try again.

Each try changes the words but not the decision, and nothing gets saved. When you need a second image to match the first, you’re back at the start.

Hazen takes the decisions out of the prompt.

It’s five skills I built for Claude Code in 2026. Two of them settle what the image should say before anything gets made. A third turns that into every craft choice it needs: light, lens, color, and what to leave out. The last two pick a model that can do the job, and save a file for every run.

That saved file is the important part. The next image in the set starts from a decision you can still read, not one you half remember.

Hazen is named after Ibn al-Haytham, whose first name, al-Hasan, reached Europe as Alhazen. He was the first to show that we see because light travels from things into the eye. He proved it with instruments he built, and wrote down what they showed. Optics starts with him, and so does the habit of keeping a record.

Five skills, each with one job.

Most AI workflows ask one prompt to do three jobs at once. Splitting them up lets each skill do one thing well, and say so when it can’t.

How a job moves through it

  1. 1

    Think

    I bring a problem, not a prompt.

    I describe it however it’s sitting in my head. A small question gets answered on the spot. A big one goes to the deep skill, and we talk it through until I have an answer I can defend.

  2. 2

    Direct

    The decision becomes instructions.

    Only then does anything get made. One skill writes out every craft choice the decision needs, and the next picks a model that can actually do the job. I don’t write prompts anymore.

  3. 3

    Make & keep

    Every run gets saved.

    Each run is saved with its prompt, settings and real cost. That’s why the second image in a set matches the first without a rewrite, whether it’s a poster or a five-second loop.

hazen-brainstorm-light

For small design questions that need a straight answer, fast.

It won’t give you an average answer. If its reply would work for any brand, it hasn’t answered yet. So it picks one option, gives a real reason, and tells you what that choice costs.

“Fine-line illustrated bean, or the name in heavy block letters?”

A clean modern sans. Approachable and contemporary, works well for a coffee shop.

The name in block letters. Your logo’s first real job is a 60-pixel circle in a delivery app and a stamp on a paper cup. Fine lines disappear at both.

  • Short answers: the call, the reason, the trade-off.
  • Makes sensible assumptions instead of asking you questions back.
  • Never says “it depends” without saying on what, and then it picks anyway.
  • Hands the question up when it turns into strategy.

hazen-brainstorming

For the big questions: what the brand stands for, which direction to take, and honest critique. The most valuable skill here.

It questions the brief before answering it. A brief rarely says what it’s really about. So it works out what the brand is claiming, and against whom, then builds every visual choice on that. If two options could swap places without changing the business, they’re moods, not directions.

ChaosLoud kids welcome. Kills premium.
GenerosityAbundance, done well. Backed.
TraditionRecipes and generations. Crowded.
OccasionThe birthday place. An event, not a habit.
  • Two or three real directions, never ten, and it tells you which one it would back.
  • Asks one question, the one an experienced designer would ask.
  • Reads a whole board of references as one system.
  • Says what each direction costs you, not only what it wins.

hazen-prompt-optimizer

The middle of the system. It turns a decision into instructions a model can follow: light, lens, color, camera position, and what must never appear.

It makes the calls for you. Asking you what lighting you want hands the work straight back. So it decides, shows its reasons line by line, and you argue with the reasons instead of the image. Good direction means choosing and ruling things out, not piling on nice adjectives.

lenskey light falloffdepth of field palettematerials subject positionnegative space finish era?crop? exclusions?
  • Names the overall look first, which settles most choices at once and keeps the prompt short.
  • Covers every craft choice. Leave three blank, like the dashed ones above, and the model makes up three of its own.
  • Ends with what to leave out. That line saves more re-rolls than any adjective.
  • Rewrites it in the words the chosen model understands best.

hazen-generate-img

Picks the model and runs it. It checks what a model can do before what it costs, rewrites the prompt for that model, and saves a record of every run.

It aims to get the first try right, not to find the cheapest model. Every image model costs between $0.003 and $0.12 a run, so the model was never the real cost. The re-rolls were. Need readable headline text? Then the models that can’t do lettering are out, however cheap they are.

hazen-generate-video

The same discipline for video, which goes wrong in its own ways: aimless drifting, everything moving at once, three actions crammed into five seconds.

It spends the one beat carefully, and says what stays still. Five seconds fits one action, so it picks the one that matters. Then it lists everything that must not move, the most useful line in video direction. Here it is, straight from the file:

Camera drifts forward through the field at an extremely slow, steady, barely perceptible speed; the nearest out-of-focus points pass the lens and leave frame. Everything else holds still - field density, the lavender bloom position, the palette, and the empty lower-left darkness. No flicker, no new elements entering, no colour shift.

  • Starts from an approved image, not from text, so the first frame is already right.
  • Spends the whole clip on one beat instead of three half-finished ones.
  • Says what must not move, which stops logos and labels from warping.
  • Video prices span 260 times what image prices do, so this discipline is also the budget.

Three things it won’t do

A system that answers everything with confidence is hard to trust. So part of the design is knowing when to say “I can’t.”

  • It won’t answer past its depth.

    When a quick question turns into strategy, the quick skill says so and hands it up.

  • It won’t quietly swap things.

    If no model can do what you asked, it says so and offers a workaround. A silent swap is the mistake you find weeks later, in print.

  • It won’t pass off a guess as a fact.

    It quotes the price before it spends and saves the real cost after. Every measured number on this page comes from the second one.

Meja

A restaurant brief that argues with itself.

The restaurant wants to feel premium and family friendly at once. Those pull in opposite directions, and a model will happily average them. This is a worked example I ran end to end for this page.

  1. “It should feel luxurious and premium, but it has to be family friendly too. Warm, you know?”

    Premium means holding back. Family means noise. Type that straight into a generator and nine design decisions are left blank, so the model fills them all in itself.

    Typed as is

  2. hazen-brainstorm-lightQuick answers

    I started with a quick question about materials. It answered, then stopped me.

    Me

    Restaurant interior, premium but family friendly. Should I go walnut or oak?

    hazen-brainstorm-light

    Oak. Walnut feels like a steakhouse and makes a room feel adults only. Oak ages into character instead of looking damaged. The catch is that oak is softer, so the tables will mark.

    But this is bigger than a quick call. Premium and family friendly are pulling against each other. Until you decide which one leads, any material answer is a guess. Take it to the deep skill.

    Premium

    • Restraint
    • Formal
    • Adult
    vs

    Family

    • Welcome
    • Generous
    • Durable

    Unresolved. The brief wants both at once.

    The two sides want opposite rooms, so oak or walnut isn’t really a question about wood.

    Spotting a conflict is quick. Solving it isn’t. So it hands the question up instead of picking a side it can’t defend.

  3. hazen-brainstormingThe big questions

    So I handed it up. This skill won’t let you keep a contradiction, and it starts by questioning the brief.

    Me

    Fine. Premium and family friendly, and I cannot drop either. How do I hold both?

    hazen-brainstorming

    “Family friendly” is a category, not a position. Which family, and what is this place claiming? There are four answers, and they’re four different businesses.

    One question before I pick: what’s the average spend per head? That decides whether tables turn twice a night or once, and a room built for one sitting looks nothing like a room built for two.

    Four directions, four different businesses

    It doesn’t argue from taste. It makes pictures: four directions at $0.004 each, so the argument happens against something you can see. Then it reads them as a set: which one keeps the premium feel, which one loses it, and which one is true but already taken.

    PremiumFamily
    • Restraint
    • Formal
    • Adult
    • Welcome
    • Generous
    • Durable

    Resolved. It leans family and brings premium along.

    Family as generosity

    Premium stops meaning “hold back” and starts meaning abundance, done well. The table is full, the room is alive, and the luxury is in the food and the room, not in how quiet everyone has to be.

  4. hazen-prompt-optimizerDecision into instructions

    One decision, passed down. Here it turns into something a model can follow.

    Me

    hazen-prompt-optimizer

    Half of that is noise. 8k, ultra detailed, hyperrealistic, masterpiece, award winning, trending on artstation give the model nothing to act on. Warm and high end but family friendly are the only parts doing any work, and they fight each other.

    So I’m reading it as premium and warm, indoors, mid-service, and deciding the rest out loud: 35mm at f/2.2 from standing eye level, low pendant lights plus one candle on the table, diners behind still readable in the blur, a server crossing the foreground, and the family mid-meal, not posed.

    No quality words at the end. The last line lists what to leave out, and that’s the line doing the work.

    The direction it wrote

    A general chatbot can rewrite a prompt. It can’t do this. This skill works from a fixed checklist. Every craft choice is either made out loud or marked as left to the model, and it always ends with what must not appear. A chatbot gives you a nicer paragraph. This gives you a recipe you can reuse for the next image in the set.

  5. hazen-generate-imgPicks a model, renders, saves

    Same brief, two starting points. One left nine decisions blank. The other had a written direction.

    The model was never the cost. The re-rolls were.

    Needs
    Photoreal light

    The room has to feel like a real place: warm light falloff, open oak grain, matte glaze. A model can either do that or it can’t.

    Chosen
    flux-2/flex, 2K

    It handles soft, gradual light and puts the focus exactly where you want it. The cheapest models flatten both.

    Cost
    $0.12 a run

    Quoted before it spent anything, then saved with the real cost after.

    $0.004 × 12 re-rolls on a model that flattens the light = $0.048 and nothing usable.
    $0.12 once, on a model that can do it = the image that shipped.

simpul

A key visual for an idea you can’t photograph.

A network for practitioners, with nothing to take a picture of. What it stood for was already settled, so the whole problem was craft: show “network” without a circuit board, a hexagon or a glowing globe. Another worked example I ran end to end for this page.

  1. “We need a key visual that says we are a network. Premium, technical, not corporate.”

    With nothing to photograph, it’s tempting to describe the idea and let the model draw it. Most people do, which is why every tech community online looks the same.

    Typed as is

  2. hazen-brainstormingThe big questions

    This one skipped the quick skill. There was no small call to make, only a question about what the image should say.

    Me

    Key visual for a practitioner network. Everything I generate looks like a stock constellation. What am I doing wrong?

    hazen-brainstorming

    You’re drawing the network from outside, like a diagram. That’s a picture of the concept, and every competitor has the same one.

    Put the viewer inside it. Inside a network there’s depth, things in front of other things, and things passing close to the lens. It stops being a drawing of connection and starts to feel like being connected. That makes it a photography problem, and photography can be directed.

    Four attempts, each one fixing the last

    The idea never changed between them. Three or four lines of the prompt did, and each change was written down. Attempt 3 ends with no dandelion or firework shapes, the fix for attempt 2, saved as a rule instead of remembered. The idea was right on the first try. The craft took four.

  3. hazen-prompt-optimizerDecision into instructions

    “Inside the network” is a decision, not an instruction. Turning it into one is this skill’s whole job.

    Me

    Key visual. Inside the field of points, deep violet, room for a headline bottom left.

    hazen-prompt-optimizer

    I’m treating this as a photo, not a drawing. 85mm at f/1.4, so the nearest points blur into soft discs and one layer in the middle stays sharp. The color goes in as a hex code, because the word violet drifts toward gray. The points thin out toward the lower left, which leaves the empty space your layout needs.

    Then nine things to leave out, including the dandelion clusters that ruined the last try.

    The prompt that worked

    Two things a general chatbot wouldn’t have done. It asked for an empty lower-left third before any layout existed, because it knows the image has a job to do on a page. And it put the list of what to leave out at the end, worded the way that model respects, so the fix stuck on the next run.

  4. hazen-generate-imgPicks a model, renders, saves

    Cheap to explore, with money spent only where it counts. The test images cost less than a cent together.

    Test on the cheapest model that can answer the question. Spend once the answer is right.

    Test
    z-image, $0.004

    Two looks, forty seconds apart, just to see if the idea was worth paying for. $0.008 for the pair.

    Final images
    flux-2/flex, 2K

    Soft, gradual light and focus you can control. Nothing in the frame needs lettering, so lettering models weren’t considered.

    Whole set
    $1.6505

    Four images, a five-second loop and every rejected try, across twelve runs.

    Twelve runs: four images and the clip used, and a written reason for the other seven.

    One direction, three variations, three files. Each is the same prompt with a few lines changed: where the quiet space falls, and how dense the field is. The set matches because it came from one decision, not three tries at the same mood.

    The mirrored one took two runs. The first let the points pile into a ridge, so no arc, no ridge, no crest went into the prompt, and the second run held. That fix is now a line in a file, not something I have to remember.

  5. hazen-generate-videoThe same, for video

    The image had to move for the site header. Not turn into something else, just breathe.

    Me

    Animate the hero plate for a looping header. Subtle.

    hazen-generate-video

    Image to video, not text to video, so the image you approved is the first frame and can’t drift. One beat only: the camera moves forward through the field, almost too slowly to see.

    Then the line that does the real work: everything else holds still. Field density, the bloom position, the palette and the dark lower left. No flicker, nothing new coming in, no color shift.

    Image to video, one beat

    Fifty-six cents, the most expensive line in the project. Video is where cost really matters. Image prices span about eleven cents from cheapest to most expensive, and video prices span 260 times that. So the rule is one clip, one beat, and a written list of what must not move.

    Eleven renders gave four images worth shipping, one clip, and a written reason for the other seven.

Where I stay in charge.

It’s fast and opinionated, and it will happily make a wrong idea look great. These are the four places I keep the final say.

  • The taste is mine, and it’s written down.

    Every rule in it is a preference I argued myself into, then wrote down. That’s why the output is consistent. Someone else’s version would be just as consistent about something completely different. There’s no neutral art direction.

  • I decide if the idea is worth making.

    It will direct a wrong idea perfectly and never mention it. In Project 1 it rendered family as chaos exactly as asked, and a chaotic room kills the premium half of the brief. Noticing that was my job, and the saved direction gives me something solid to check it against.

  • I make the final pick.

    Eleven renders gave four images worth shipping. Choosing wasn’t automated. It got faster and better informed. Someone still has to look at attempt 2 and see the dandelions, then write down what to rule out next time.

  • I set the rules it follows.

    Hand it up instead of making it up. Say when a model can’t do something instead of quietly swapping. Quote a price before spending and save the real one after. I wrote those rules, and they’re why I trust it when it does sound confident.

What I actually built here.

Three things I’d take from this. None of them are really about AI.

  1. I found the real problem.

    The models were never the problem. The gap between a decision and an instruction was. That’s a design systems problem in different clothes, and I recognized it because I’ve spent six years solving it for developers.

  2. I wrote my judgment down.

    Not as a style guide nobody opens. As rules with reasons, written so something with no taste of its own can follow them without me in the room, and push back when it should.

  3. I made it accountable.

    Every run leaves a record, so the work can be checked, not just admired. Two projects, twenty-five files, $2.63. Every measured number on this page comes from those files.

A component library stores decisions so a developer who wasn’t in the room still ships something on brand. Hazen stores decisions so a model with no taste of its own still makes work I’ll put my name on. Same problem, different worker.

It started because I kept losing afternoons to re-rolling. A small, unglamorous, very real problem. It ended up as infrastructure: a method, a set of rules, and a record that outlives the session.

That’s what I’d bring to a team, and it has very little to do with AI.

Like its namesake, Hazen’s real output is a method, and people trained well enough to carry it.

Both projects here are worked examples. The brands are mine, and the briefs are realistic, not real. The runs are real.
